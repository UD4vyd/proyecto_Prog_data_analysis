1. Project Overview

Title. Extreme Weather Monitoring System — Texas.

Motivation. Texas concentrates almost every type of extreme weather recorded in the United States: heat waves, hard freezes (the February 2021 winter storm), flash floods, large hail, tornadoes, damaging thunderstorm winds, dust storms and winter storms. In 2025 alone the NOAA Storm Events Database lists 5,641 Texas records. The information needed to judge whether today's weather is unusual is public, but it is spread over services with different formats, time conventions, identifiers and update rhythms: bulk CSV archives that are recompiled months later, a daily climate archive with a three-day delay, and real-time APIs that only keep a few days of data.

Goal. From a data-engineering perspective: build a reproducible pipeline that loads 2010–present history from two bulk/historical sources, polls two operational NWS services incrementally with watermarks, and integrates the four into one canonical PostgreSQL/PostGIS model with validation, quarantine, deduplication and cross-source event grouping. From an analytical perspective: compare each current measurement with the historical distribution of the same station and season and classify it as NORMAL / UNUSUAL / EXTREME, supported by active NWS alerts and by the impact history of the same county.

Scope. State of Texas; 10 first-order ASOS stations (KDFW, KIAH, KAUS, KSAT, KELP, KLBB, KAMA, KCRP, KMAF, KBRO) that are published both by GHCN-Daily and by the NWS API; all Storm Events of Texas counties and forecast zones; all NWS alerts with area=TX. Historical window 2010-01-01 → present.

Explicit exclusions. No forecasting or prediction of hazards; no numerical weather models or radar/satellite imagery; no states other than Texas; no marine zones beyond what NWS includes in area=TX; no damage estimation; no individual-level or personal data.

Constraints. Free and open sources only; US-only services (hence US scope); NWS requires a descriptive User-Agent; the observations endpoint returns at most 500 features; GHCN-Daily has a ~3-day publication delay and Storm Events several months; local execution with Docker Compose; all documentation in English.

2. Hazard Definition

Phenomenon. Extreme weather is weather at the tails of the local climatological distribution — values that are rare for that place and that time of year. Team 5 monitors seven hazard components:

ComponentHow it is measuredSeverity measure and unitSources
Extreme heatDaily maximum temperature; instantaneous temperature and heat indexTMAX, heat index (°C)GHCN-Daily, NWS obs, Storm Events (Heat / Excessive Heat), NWS heat alerts
Extreme coldDaily minimum temperature; wind chillTMIN, wind chill (°C)GHCN-Daily, NWS obs, Storm Events (Cold/Wind Chill, Extreme Cold, Frost/Freeze), freeze alerts
Heavy precipitation / flash flood24-h precipitation accumulation; 1-h ratePRCP (mm/day), precipitation 1 h (mm)GHCN-Daily, NWS obs, Storm Events (Heavy Rain, Flash Flood), flood alerts
Severe windFastest 5-second gust; measured/estimated gust in reportsWSF5, gust (m/s); Storm Events knots → m/sGHCN-Daily, NWS obs, Storm Events (Thunderstorm/High/Strong Wind), wind alerts
HailSpotter/observer hail sizeDiameter (in → mm)Storm Events, Severe Thunderstorm Warnings
TornadoDamage surveyEF scale (0–5)Storm Events, Tornado Watch/Warning
Winter stormDaily snowfall and snow depthSNOW, SNWD (mm)GHCN-Daily, Storm Events (Winter Storm, Heavy Snow, Ice Storm), winter alerts

Alerts carry an ordinal severity (CAP: Minor 1, Moderate 2, Severe 3, Extreme 4). Storm Events without a physical magnitude receive an impact level 0–3 derived from casualties and damage (§8).

Why it matters. Heat is the leading weather-related cause of death in the US, freezes stress the Texas power grid (February 2021), and Texas is among the states with the most flood fatalities, most of them in flash floods. Knowing early that a measurement is unusual for the station and season helps emergency managers decide on cooling/warming centres, public messaging and resource staging.

Geographic scope and justification. Texas is mandatory-compatible with all four sources (Storm Events and NWS are US services; GHCN-Daily's densest coverage is the US). Ten first-order stations give a manageable volume (≈62,000 station-days for 2010–2026) while covering the climatic diversity of the state: humid Gulf coast (KIAH, KCRP, KBRO), central Texas flash-flood alley (KAUS, KSAT), north Texas (KDFW), Panhandle and South Plains (KAMA, KLBB), Permian Basin (KMAF) and the Chihuahuan desert (KELP, Mountain Time).

Historical window. 2010-01-01 → present (≥ 16 years, above the five required). Sixteen years give ≈ 500 values per station for a ±15-day seasonal window, enough to estimate the 99th percentile; going further back would add station relocations and longer-term climate trends that bias a "current climate" baseline, without improving the tail estimate much.

Current situation. (a) NWS alerts active now for Texas; (b) station observations of the last 24 hours (rolling max/min temperature, 24-h precipitation, max gust) compared with the baseline; (c) the last 7 days of alerts and observations for context. Storm Events and GHCN-Daily are too delayed to define the current situation; they provide the baseline and the impact history.

4. Source Profiling

The notebook notebooks/01_source_profiling.ipynb profiles every source (≥ 200 records each): null percentage per field, value ranges, duplicated identifiers, timestamp format and time zone, coordinate validity and unit consistency, and applies the draft canonical mapping. Summary after mapping:

$prof_tbl

Main observations: no duplicated native identifiers in any source; Storm Events uses local standard time per county/zone (CST-6 for 98% of rows, MST-7 for El Paso and Hudspeth counties/zones); 36.7% of Storm Events rows (all zone-based, CZ_TYPE='Z') have no coordinates; GHCN-Daily completeness is 99.9–100% for the ten stations with no TMIN>TMAX inconsistencies; NWS alerts use local offsets (-06:00); NWS observations are already UTC, but 96% of wind-gust values and 97% of hourly precipitation values are null.

Volume estimate and partitioning. Storm Events 2010–2026: 17 yearly files of 8.6–15.0 MB gz (≈ 186 MB US-wide, ≈ 96,000 Texas rows at ≈ 5,600 per year) → partition by year. GHCN-Daily: ≈ 62,000 station-days × 8 elements (≈ 500,000 measurements, ≈ 28 MB JSON) → 170 station-year requests. NWS alerts ≈ 11,600 messages/year for Texas. NWS observations ≈ 450,000 observations/year for 10 stations (~5-min reporting) → polled every 30 min per station.

5. Data-Quality Findings

Thirteen concrete problems were found (five required); each has a real example record from the samples. The full table is saved as workshop_1/data_quality_issues.csv.

$dq_tbl
Most relevant for the design: (1) silent truncation of NWS observations at 500 features — a naive "last 7 days" request loses 40% of the window, so the incremental poll must be frequent and detect full pages; (2) a GHCN-Daily WSF5 of 76.0 m/s (~148 kt) at Lubbock on 2025-06-17 with a blank QC flag, while Storm Events reports a 50-kt measured gust at the same county that day: the validation layer needs physical-plausibility ranges in addition to source QC flags; (3) zone-based Storm Events have neither coordinates nor county FIPS, so matching requires a zone lookup table, never name matching (HARRIS also matches HARRISON).

6. Incremental Behaviour — Two Polls at Least One Hour Apart

The incremental sources were polled twice with the same requests; identifiers, content hashes and HTTP caching headers were compared (notebook §8).

$poll_tbl

How new or modified records can be detected. NWS alerts: every message has a stable, globally unique id; an alert never changes in place — it evolves through new Update/Cancel messages that list superseded ids in references, and leaves /alerts/active when it expires. Detection therefore uses new ids since the watermark on sent, plus the reference chains. NWS observations: the key is (stationId, timestamp); the API accepts a start parameter, which is the natural "updated since" filter. Caching headers are only partly useful: /alerts/active returns a weak ETag with max-age=5, which allows a conditional request (If-None-Match) to skip an unchanged active set, but it says nothing about which records changed; the observations endpoint and the NCEI Access Data Service return neither ETag nor Last-Modified. Record-level content hashes are therefore the change signal.

Revisions and deletions. Storm Events recompiles whole years long after publication (the 2012 file was recompiled on 2026-10-26); GHCN-Daily station-days are re-published from another source stream about a year later (source flag W before Aug-2025, 1 afterwards); NWS observations can receive late QC; alerts are never edited but disappear from the active set. Consequence for the watermark: it is never a strict ">" filter — each source re-reads an overlap window (2 h alerts, 3 h observations, 45 days + monthly 13-month re-read GHCN, whole year Storm Events) and the content hash decides between insert, update or no-op.

7. Cross-Source Overlap

Rule used for candidates (refined in Workshop 3): Storm Events record in the county of a station (exact CZ_FIPS for county rows; word-boundary name match for zone rows) and on the same local calendar day as the GHCN-Daily record, with a compatible variable. The rule produced $n_pairs candidate pairs for 2025; the strongest pair per event type is listed:

$pairs_tbl

What differs. Time: Storm Events gives a local begin time to the minute, GHCN a local-standard day; matching must happen on the local day, not the UTC day. Position: zone/county polygons versus a station point (zone rows have no coordinates). Severity: knots versus m/s and estimated (EG) versus measured (MG) gusts — the High Wind pair at Potter County (72 kt MG = 37.0 m/s vs WSF5 = 37.1 m/s) agrees within 0.1 m/s, whereas the Lubbock Thunderstorm Wind pair (50 kt vs 76.0 m/s) exposes the GHCN outlier of §5. Heat/cold/flood reports have no magnitude, so the station value is the only physical measurement. Identifier: no shared key at all.

Second overlap — NWS observations ↔ GHCN-Daily. The same ASOS sensors feed both services. For complete local days, the maximum of the NWS observations matches GHCN TMAX with a mean absolute difference of 0.11 °C over 9 complete station-days when days are cut at local standard midnight, versus 0.22 °C when the UTC day is used (notebook §7) — confirming both the station pairing and the local-standard-day rule of §8; incomplete days at the start of the 500-record window must be excluded, another consequence of the truncation. Third overlap — alert chains: 105 of 224 alerts reference earlier messages of the same event (Update/Cancel), so even within one source several records describe one real-world event.

Correctness if a run fails halfway. (1) The raw response is written to the landing zone (source_YYYYMMDD_HHMMSS.ext) before parsing, so it can be replayed. (2) For each source, canonical rows, domain rows, quarantined rows and the new watermark are committed in a single transaction; if the process dies, the transaction rolls back and the watermark keeps its last successful value. (3) The next run re-requests the same interval plus the overlap; deterministic event_ids and INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE WHERE content_hash differs make the repetition harmless. (4) Historical partitions are logged when committed, so a restart resumes from the first unfinished year/station-year. (5) The failed ingestion_run keeps status='failed' and the error, visible in --mode status and in the dashboard's data-health panel. Details: docs/ingestion_contract.md.

10. Functional User

Role. Emergency-management analyst in a Texas county Office of Emergency Management (or a regional office of the Texas Division of Emergency Management).

Profile. Not a meteorologist; comfortable with dashboards and maps; works shifts; reads NWS products but has no time to cross-check several websites; must brief decision makers twice a day and justify recommendations with facts.

Decisions supported. Whether to open cooling or warming centres; whether to raise the county's readiness level or pre-position resources (flood barricades, crews); what to say in public messaging ("hottest night for this time of year in 15 years"); whether data are fresh enough to trust before a briefing.

Why the dashboard is useful. It puts in one screen what is happening now (alerts + latest observations), whether it is unusual for this place and season (percentile-based level), what happened in the past in the same county (Storm Events impacts) and how fresh and complete the data are — without claiming to predict anything.

12. Indicator Proposal — Extreme Weather Level (NORMAL / UNUSUAL / EXTREME)

Inputs. (a) Baseline: GHCN-Daily 2010 → last complete year for each station and element (TMAX, TMIN, PRCP, WSF5, SNOW), using a ±15-day window around each day of the year (≈ 31 days × 16 years ≈ 500 values) → percentiles p1, p5, p10, p90, p95, p99 and record values. (b) Current values: from NWS observations, rolling 24-h maximum and minimum temperature, 24-h precipitation sum and maximum gust; from GHCN, the latest published day. (c) Active NWS alerts whose zones contain the station.

Rule (parameterised; defaults shown). For "high-tail" elements (TMAX, PRCP, WSF5, SNOW): NORMAL if value < p90; UNUSUAL if p90 ≤ value < p99; EXTREME if value ≥ p99 or above the seasonal record. For TMIN the mirror rule applies (≤ p10 UNUSUAL, ≤ p1 EXTREME). Precipitation percentiles are computed on wet days only (PRCP ≥ 1 mm) to avoid a distribution dominated by zeros. Alert escalation: if an active alert of the matching type (e.g., Extreme Heat Warning, Freeze/Hard Freeze Warning, Flash Flood Warning, High Wind Warning) covers the station, the level is raised one step (capped at EXTREME) and marked "official alert". Output per station × component: level, percentile rank, alert flag; per region: the worst component level and the number of stations at each level.

Validation against historical events. Back-test the rule on 2010–2025 daily GHCN values with a leave-one-year-out baseline, and compare with Storm Events of the matching type in the station's county/zone on the same local day: hit rate = share of Storm Events days of that type flagged UNUSUAL or EXTREME; false-alarm share = share of EXTREME days without any matching report; plus case studies (February 2021 winter storm, summer 2023 heat, 2025 central Texas floods). A sensitivity table over p90/p95/p97.5 and p99/p99.5 thresholds will be reported (Workshop 4).

Limitations. Percentiles describe rarity, not impact; ten stations do not represent every county; Storm Events reports are biased toward populated areas; GHCN-Daily and the NWS day definitions differ (local standard day vs UTC), which is handled explicitly; the indicator is a monitoring aid and does not predict hazards.

13. Risks and Mitigation

#RiskLikelihood / impactMitigation
R1Source outage (NWS API 5xx, NCEI maintenance)Medium / HighRetries with exponential back-off and timeouts; watermark advances only after commit; raw cache allows re-processing; data-health panel shows watermark age.

15. Repository

The repository follows the structure required by the workshop: src/ (sources, normalization, storage), ingest.py (CLI stub with the final arguments), config/sources.yaml (one entry per source with URL, format, schedule and watermark field), data/raw/ (git-ignored landing zone), sql/ (draft schema), dashboard/, tests/ and tests/fixtures/ (recorded real responses, 17 passing tests), notebooks/01_source_profiling.ipynb (executed), docs/ (this report, ingestion contract, source status log), workshop_1/ (mapping.md, samples, scripts, data-quality table), .env.example, .gitignore and README.md.
